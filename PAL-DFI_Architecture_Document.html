01

Executive Summary

PAL-DFI is a national trust infrastructure built to give Palestinians a secure, privacy-respecting digital identity they actually own. Rather than centralizing sensitive data in a single government database, the network lets citizens hold their own credentials in a mobile wallet and share only what a specific transaction requires.

The platform connects financial institutions, government agencies, and telecommunications providers through a blockchain-governed trust framework operated by the Palestine Monetary Authority. When a bank needs to confirm that a customer is who they say they are, it doesn't need to call the Ministry of Interior directly. It checks a cryptographic proof the citizen presents from their wallet, validated against the blockchain's trust layer. No raw data changes hands unless the citizen agrees.

Technically, PAL-DFI runs on Hyperledger Fabric and fully adopts W3C standards for Decentralized Identifiers and Verifiable Credentials. Protocol-level interoperability is handled through OpenID4VCI, OpenID4VP, OAuth2, and OpenID Connect, so institutions don't need to rework their existing systems to participate.

02

Architecture Principles

Every decision in this architecture traces back to a small set of commitments. They aren't aspirational statements — they shape what gets built and what doesn't.

Trust by Design

Trust is earned through cryptographic verification and approved participant status, not through assumed relationships between institutions.

Privacy by Design

No citizen personal information lives on the blockchain. The chain records hashes and status flags, nothing that identifies a person.

Data Minimization

Verifiers receive only the specific attributes a transaction requires. A bank confirming residency doesn't receive date of birth unless it asks, and the citizen must consent.

Decentralized Trust

Each participant keeps ownership of their own source systems. PAL-DFI provides shared infrastructure for trust — not a centralized data store.

Regulatory Governance

PMA governs participation, trust policies, and compliance across the network. Clear lines of authority prevent ambiguity about who approves what.

Standards-Based Interoperability

All integrations must comply with W3C DID, W3C VC, OpenID4VCI, and OpenID4VP. Proprietary integrations are not permitted at the protocol level.

03

Blockchain Architecture Overview

The blockchain is the trust layer of PAL-DFI, not its data store. It answers one type of question reliably: can this credential be trusted right now? Everything else — the credential itself, the citizen's identity details, transaction records — lives off-chain in the appropriate institutional systems.

Specifically, the blockchain handles trust management, credential status verification, issuer and verifier governance, revocation tracking, and immutable audit anchoring. It deliberately does not hold citizen identity data or credential content. That boundary is fundamental to the privacy model.

04

Blockchain Governance Model

The Palestine Monetary Authority owns and operates the network. That isn't a formality — PMA has final say on who can join, what policies govern participation, and when smart contracts get deployed or updated. In practice this means a new institution wanting to issue credentials must apply to PMA, satisfy its compliance requirements, and receive explicit approval before its node joins the network.

PMA's governance responsibilities cover network participation decisions, trust policy definition, audit supervision, and compliance oversight. The concentration of authority is intentional: decentralized identity infrastructure still needs a regulated entity accountable for the rules of the road.

05

Network Design

PAL-DFI runs on Hyperledger Fabric as a permissioned consortium blockchain. Not every blockchain suits a regulated national identity network — permissioned architecture means that unknown participants cannot join, and every transaction can be traced to an approved organization.

Consensus is handled through RAFT, chosen for its proven reliability in high-availability environments and its compatibility with the regulatory oversight requirements here. The network is designed for transaction immutability, controlled participation, and the kind of audit transparency a central bank needs.

Network Channels

Hyperledger Fabric channels allow different subsets of participants to transact privately within the same network. PAL-DFI uses three channels with tightly defined purposes and membership.

Channel Purpose Participants
Governance Channel Network-level governance operations — policy updates, participant decisions PMA only
Trust Channel Participant trust management — issuer and verifier registration PMA, MOI, Jawwal, Banks
Credential Status Channel Credential verification support — status checks and revocation queries All approved organizations

09

Trust Registry

The Trust Registry is the list of who is permitted to do what in the network. Before any institution can issue or verify credentials, it must appear here in an active state. The registry exposes functions to register, suspend, and reactivate both issuers and verifiers, giving PMA direct control over participation status.

Stored Data

  • Participant ID
  • Participant DID
  • Status
  • Approval Date
10

DID Registry

The DID Registry anchors decentralized identifiers to the blockchain without recording anything personally identifiable. When a citizen's wallet creates a DID, a public key hash and status flag are written on-chain. That's enough for a verifier to confirm cryptographic authenticity without ever knowing the citizen's name, address, or any attribute.

Stored Data

  • DID
  • Public Key Hash
  • Status
  • Creation Timestamp
11

Credential & Revocation Registries

The Credential Registry doesn't store credentials — it stores proof that a credential was legitimately issued. When a verifier wants to confirm a presentation hasn't been tampered with, it checks the credential's hash against this registry. The actual credential content stays in the citizen's wallet.

Credential Registry — Stored Data

  • Credential Hash
  • Issuer DID
  • Credential Type
  • Issue Date
  • Status

The Revocation Registry tracks credentials that have been invalidated — a national ID that was replaced after loss, a KYC credential revoked after fraud detection. Any verifier can check revocation status in real time without contacting the original issuer.

Revocation Registry — Stored Data

  • Credential Hash
  • Revocation Status
  • Reason Code
  • Timestamp
12

Audit Architecture

Regulatory accountability requires a record that cannot be altered after the fact. The Audit Contract writes cryptographic anchors of significant events to the blockchain — not the event data itself, but a hash that can later confirm whether off-chain audit logs have been tampered with. PMA uses these anchors during investigations and compliance reviews.

Stored Data

  • Audit Anchor ID
  • Hash
  • Source System
  • Timestamp

Data Architecture Overview

PAL-DFI's data architecture is deliberately fragmented — not because fragmentation is convenient, but because it is what privacy requires. There is no single database where a complete picture of any citizen could be reconstructed. Different data domains live with their natural owners, and the blockchain holds only what is needed to verify trust status.

Data Domains

  • Identity Domain
  • DID Domain
  • Credential Domain
  • Verification Domain
  • Consent Domain
  • Trust Domain
  • Governance Domain
  • Audit Domain
15

Key Data Domains

Identity Domain — Owned by the Ministry of Interior. National ID, citizen name, date of birth, and nationality live exclusively within MOI systems and are never replicated to the blockchain or any shared repository.

Credential Domain — Owned by the respective issuers. National Identity Credentials come from MOI, SIM Ownership Credentials from Jawwal, KYC and Account Ownership Credentials from banks. Metadata such as issue date, expiry, and type is shared; credential content is not.

Consent Domain — Citizen-controlled. Before any credential attribute is shared with a verifier, a consent record is created capturing who requested it, for what purpose, and when the consent expires. Citizens can review and withdraw consent.

Trust and Governance Domains — Both owned and operated by PMA. These cover issuer and verifier registry data, governance policies, compliance rules, and network configuration. They are the operational core of PMA's regulatory function.

17

Data Lifecycle & Privacy

Every credential follows a defined lifecycle: creation, validation, storage, active use, revocation, archiving, and eventual deletion. All transitions generate auditable events. There is no undefined state where data sits in limbo outside the governance model.

🔒

Privacy Commitments

There is no centralized citizen identity repository. No biometric data is stored on-chain. Credential contents never touch the blockchain. Selective disclosure is used wherever technically possible. Citizens must give explicit consent before any credential attribute is shared with a third party.

End-to-End Scenario

Here is what the full flow looks like for a citizen opening a bank account using PAL-DFI credentials.

1
Citizen installs the PAL-DFI Wallet on their phone.
2
The wallet generates a Decentralized Identifier (DID) locally on the device.
3
The DID's public key hash is anchored on the blockchain via the DID Registry.
4
The citizen visits Ministry of Interior, which verifies their physical identity documents.
5
MOI issues a National Identity Credential, delivered directly to the citizen's wallet.
6
The credential is stored in the wallet — not on a government server, not on the blockchain.
7
A bank requests identity verification. The citizen's wallet generates a Verifiable Presentation.
8
The bank verifies the cryptographic signature, DID status, issuer trust status, and revocation status — all without contacting MOI.
9
The verification result is recorded and an audit anchor is written to the blockchain.

Conclusion

PAL-DFI builds something Palestine has not had before — a trust infrastructure where financial institutions, government agencies, and telecommunications providers can verify citizen credentials reliably, without needing to call each other or maintain shared databases of sensitive information.

The architecture keeps the PMA in clear regulatory control, gives citizens ownership of their own credentials, and uses open international standards so the network can grow without locking anyone into proprietary technology.

The result is a foundation for a Palestinian digital economy built on genuine trust — not on assumptions.
